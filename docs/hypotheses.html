Hypotheses & conjectures · a register that can change

Explicit claims.
No ready-made verdict.

Each hypothesis is written so that it could fail. “Not supported” is not the same as “disproved”: every verdict is tied to a model, a dataset and a way of cutting the text into units.

The register

Ten hypotheses, ten verdicts.

H01Disfavoured

The herbal text is Latin or Italian in a simple cipher

One glyph per letter, spaces preserved, under our 23 glyph units.

The solver recovers both controls and finds no reading in the Voynich. Triple glyphs that a Latin plaintext could not produce. The search is heuristic, so not exhaustively falsified.

H02Contradicted

There are no repeatable local rules

The text would then be close to random at the glyph level.

Models trained on some pages predict other pages far better than glyph frequencies alone.

H03Contradicted

Local predictability proves a human language

A tempting argument often made for the Voynich.

The Seraphinianus, an invented script, is just as locally predictable. The property does not discriminate.

H04Not established

The two codices are statistically equivalent

Same kind of object, produced the same way.

Some shared patterns, some stable differences (context gain 0.001 vs 0.09 bits), and OCR uncertainty. No equivalence test was run.

H05Contradicted

Nothing beyond two preceding glyphs helps

An earlier, too-strong wording of our own result.

With connected EVA units the gain is small but positive (0.003–0.015 bits). We corrected the claim.

H06Not supported

Prefixes strongly constrain suffixes

As in languages with agreement or vowel harmony inside the word.

Combined gain about 0.001 bits per suffix in the 2-unit model, and no generalisation to unseen word forms.

H07Contradicted

A tree grammar predicts better than a chain

The tested two-state PCFG against a two-state HMM, on suffix classes.

PCFG worse by 0.0193 bits/token; 95% interval [+0.0065, +0.0343]. This rejects one model on one representation, not hierarchy in general.

H08Not justified

The Seraphinianus OCR is exact enough to count strokes

Needed to trust repeated-glyph statistics on the comparison text.

Disagreements concentrate exactly on counts of repeated strokes and on segmentation.

H09Open

The manuscript has no recoverable meaning

The hoax or glossolalia family of explanations.

No experiment here establishes this, and statistics alone cannot (see the identifiability argument on the methods page).

H10Open

The text respects or violates Moro's constraints

Structure-dependent rules that human brains learn and “impossible” linear rules they do not.

Not directly testable: we have no identified syntax, utterance boundaries or morphosyntactic units to test them on.

Conjectures under study

Ideas we are testing,
not results.

These are working leads. Each comes with the observation that motivates it and the check that could kill it.

C1 · WORD BUILDING

Words are prefix + base + suffix

Families such as okaiin / qokaiin (166 and 220 occurrences) suggest a rule word → prefix + base + suffix, or extensions X → aX and X → Xa.

Caution: a finite-state machine can generate such forms. Drawing a tree is not evidence of human structural dependency.

C2 · ALLOGRAPHY

Final m is a variant of r

Replacing line-final m by r turns 74.7% of held-out cases into known words, against 42.6% unchanged.

Weakened by controls: the best substitute changes with how uncertain spaces are handled, and labels otalar and otalam sit side by side on f70v2. Global merging abandoned for now.

C3 · UNITS

qo and iin are single units

q is followed by o 98% of the time; in, iin, iiin occur 1,601, 3,688 and 149 times.

Changing units changes every statistic on this site. Units must be learned on training pages only, then compared on the same test targets.

C4 · LAYOUT

Gallows glyphs mark paragraphs

89.4% of paragraph-initial words start with f k p t, against 8.5% of other line-initial words; 169 of 178 pages agree.

Open question: are they separate markers, like decorated initials, or part of the first word?

C5 · LABELS

Zodiac labels name things

okeodaly appears on both Taurus pages, f71v and f72r1, with different neighbours.

Both folios belong to the same bifolio, so this is one observation, not two. Near-spellings occur on 9–10 other pages.

C6 · PRODUCTION

Text was generated by a local process

The combined picture of weak long-range context, full prefix–suffix combinability and section-wise drift fits a scribe following local habits.

To test it: topic-varying generators and copy-and-edit models matched to section structure, scored on held-out pages.

The wider debate

What is known
outside this project.

A century of research has narrowed the question. These are the established facts and the main families of explanation, with the strongest evidence each side cites.

Established facts about the object

FactEvidence

FAMILY A

A natural language in an unknown script

Proposals include European, Semitic and Asian languages, often with a syllabic or abbreviating script.

For: Zipf-like word frequencies, word clustering by section (Montemurro & Zanette 2013), text that tracks illustrations. Against: very low glyph entropy, rigid word structure. Our results: section effect supports, context gain constrains.

FAMILY B

A cipher or code

A known language disguised by a verbose cipher, nomenclator or code book.

For: 15th-century Italy used ciphers widely; verbose ciphers lower entropy. Against: no plaintext recovered despite intense effort. Our results: simple substitution of Latin or Italian is disfavoured.

FAMILY C

Meaningless text, made by a procedure

A hoax, a commercial fake or a devotional exercise produced with tables or a grille.

For: Rugg (2004) reproduced Voynich-like words with a Cardan grille; Timm & Schinner (2020) generated similar text by copying and modifying earlier words. Against: the procedure would have to reproduce section effects and Currier dialects. Our results: the word machine shows how far simple rules go.

FAMILY D

Constructed language or glossolalia

An invented philosophical language, or speech-like writing produced without a fixed code.

For: regular morphology with odd distributions. Against: hard to test without units. The Seraphinianus, an artist's invented script, is the closest modern analogue and shares several statistics.

A note on claimed decipherments

New decipherments are announced almost every year. None has yet been accepted, because none predicts unseen text. Stephen Bax's provisional plant-name readings (2014) are often cited as the most careful attempt: he presented them as a starting point, not a solution.

Falsifiability

What would change our view?

Any of these would challenge a purely local account of the Voynich text.

A stable hierarchical advantage on held-out pages

A grammar with nested structure that predicts unseen pages better than the best sequential model, across codings and splits.

Independently validated segmentation

Units learned without looking at the test pages that also agree with palaeographic evidence from the images.

Long-distance dependencies beyond layout

Dependencies that survive controls for line position, page, section and scribe.

A decipherment that predicts

An externally anchored reading that generalises to new pages without ad hoc choices, and makes predictions before they are checked.

Glossary

Terms used on this site.

EVA

The European Voynich Alphabet: a convention for typing Voynich glyphs with Latin letters. ch or aiin name shapes, not sounds.

Entropy, in bits per glyph

How many yes/no questions a model needs, on average, to guess the next glyph. Lower means better prediction, not better translation.

Positive control

A test case where we know the answer. If a method fails on it, its failure on the Voynich tells us nothing.

PCFG and HMM

A probabilistic context-free grammar builds nested trees; a hidden Markov model chains hidden states left to right. Comparing them asks whether nesting adds predictive power.

Currier A and B, hands

Two statistical “dialects” of the Voynich text identified by Prescott Currier in the 1970s, and the distinct scribal hands identified by palaeographers.

Moro's constraints

Andrea Moro and colleagues showed that brains handle rules based on hierarchical structure differently from rules based on linear word order. Testing a script for this needs known syntactic units first.
