Two books, one question

Can structure alone
make a language?

Any text written by people has patterns. So do many things that carry no message at all. The hard part is telling the two apart.

The Voynich manuscript

About 240 vellum pages, radiocarbon-dated to 1404–1438, full of plants that match no known species, star charts, and small nude figures bathing in green pools. The writing is fluent and consistent, as if the scribes knew exactly what they were doing. It has resisted professional codebreakers, linguists and statisticians for more than a century.

The Codex Seraphinianus

An encyclopedia of an imaginary world, drawn and written by the Italian artist Luigi Serafini in the late 1970s. Its script looks just as systematic. Serafini has said that it hides no message. That makes it a rare control: a modern, fluent, invented script against which the Voynich can be measured.

What the pages show

Five kinds of page,
one kind of writing.

Scholars sort the manuscript by its pictures. The text changes subtly from section to section, and one of our experiments asks whether those changes follow the pictures.

Each finding below is a measured number from this project, with a control to compare it against. None of them settles the question on its own.

Toward “no message”

Findings that fit a script produced by simple local rules, with little information beyond the next sign.

0.001 bits

Longer context barely helps

Knowing three previous glyphs instead of two improves prediction by about 0.001 bits in the Voynich. In Latin and Italian the gain is about 0.33 bits, some 300 times larger.

36 of 36

Every prefix fits every ending

Six word starts (ok, qok, ot, qot, ch, sh) combine with all six common endings (aiin, ain, edy, eedy, ol, ar). That looks like a slot machine as much as a grammar.

39 vs 0

Triple letters are common

39 words in our Voynich sample contain the same glyph three times in a row. The Latin control text has none in 7,595 words. A simple letter-for-letter cipher of Latin could not produce them.

100% vs 1–7%

The cipher attack works on controls only

Our solver fully recovers Latin and Italian enciphered with a secret alphabet. Run on the Voynich, it produces no readable text.

Toward “something is encoded”

Findings that fit an organised system where words depend on context, position and subject.

87% accuracy

The text knows what the picture shows

From 30 words alone, a simple classifier tells herbal pages from bathing pages correctly for 34 of 39 pages, with scribe and dialect held fixed.

13× chance

Neighbouring words depend on each other

The last glyph of a word predicts the first glyph of the next with 0.21 bits of shared information. Shuffled lines give 0.016. Real word order matters.

89% vs 8%

Paragraphs have a shape

Nearly nine paragraph-opening words in ten begin with a tall “gallows” glyph. Elsewhere at line start the rate is 8%. Something marks where paragraphs begin.

38 vs 5.5

Some word pairs are favourites

The pair s aiin occurs 38 times where shuffling predicts 5.5. Fixed phrases like this are typical of language, though not proof of it.

Our reading of the balance. The Voynich text is highly organised, but most of that organisation is very local: within a word and between neighbouring words. The Seraphinianus, an invented script, shares several of these properties. Organisation is therefore not proof of meaning, and a weak local signal is not proof of its absence. Both columns have limits, listed on the results page.
